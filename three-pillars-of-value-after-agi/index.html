The Three Pillars of Value After AGI

18 October 2026 · Tom Bannerman · ~12 min

As the price of intelligence falls toward zero, value moves to what stays scarce: human connection, production capability and access to resources.

Intelligence is becoming free

In a world with artificial general intelligence (AGI) and then superintelligence (SI), the market value of intelligence will approach zero. Not suddenly, and not evenly, but on a curve that only goes one way.

This is not a prediction about some distant future. In October 2026, a large share of knowledge work is already cheaper to do with a machine than with a person. The price of a fixed level of AI capability falls by roughly an order of magnitude every year. No human labour market has ever had to compete with a price curve like that.

If intelligence becomes abundant, then value moves to what stays scarce. I think three things stay scarce: human connection, production capability, and access to resources. This article explains why, and what it means for where we put our time, our money and our careers.

The decline curve

The value of human cognitive work will fall along a curve set by two rates: how fast the cost of AI falls, and how fast organisations accept it. Cost sets how low the price can go. Acceptance sets how fast the market gets there.

For any task that a machine can do from digital information, the market price of that task is roughly a blend of the human cost and the machine cost:

P(t) = A(t)⁢ CAI(t) + (1−A(t))⁢ Wh

Here A(t) is the share of buyers that accept AI for the task, CAI(t) is the cost of the AI system, and Wh is the cost of the human. As CAI falls toward zero and A rises toward one, P(t) goes to zero. The human who sells that task follows the same curve.

The cost term is already collapsing. Across benchmarks, Epoch AI measured the price of a fixed level of capability falling between 9x and 900x per year. Its September 2026 update puts the trend at about 13x per year since 2023. A usage-weighted market index hit US$0.97 per million tokens in September 2026. At roughly 0.75 words per token, one dollar now buys more written output than most professionals produce in a year.

The acceptance term is the slow one, and it is the window people have. 88% of organisations use AI in at least one business function, but only 10% say it is core to how they operate. The technology is ahead of the institutions. That gap will close, and the speed at which it closes is the speed at which cognitive wages fall.

Pillar 1: Human connection

People will keep paying a premium for another human being, even when a machine does the job better and cheaper. That premium is not about the output. It is about the presence.

The coffee shop is the clearest example. A Nespresso machine at home makes a consistent coffee for less than a dollar. Cafes still fill every morning, and people pay five times as much for a cup made by a person who knows their name. The product is the coffee; the value is the human.

The same pattern shows in live music. Recorded music has been close to free for more than a decade, yet live music spending keeps rising. In the first quarter of 2026, Gen Z concertgoers in the US spent an average of US$101 per month on live music. In the UK, 88% of people surveyed by Mastercard said they would move spending from goods to lived experiences. Abundant digital content did not kill the live show. It made the live show more valuable.

This pillar covers any work where the buyer subconsciously wants a human on the other side:

  • Hospitality: cafes, restaurants, bars, hotels
  • Live performance: music, theatre, sport, comedy
  • Care: social work, aged care, nursing, childcare, counselling
  • Teaching and coaching, where the relationship drives the result
  • Community: clubs, events, places where people gather

The test is simple. If a cheaper machine version already exists and people still choose the human, the value is in the connection. Those jobs will hold their value, and may gain value, as everything around them gets cheaper.

Pillar 2: Production capability

Intelligence without hands is a consultant. The value moves to whoever can connect AGI and SI systems to the physical world, so that they can act on it: from electrons to atoms.

Production capability means two things.

First, the ability to learn from the universe directly. Today's models learn mostly from human text, and that supply is finite. The next source of knowledge is physics itself. A system that can design an experiment, run it in a real laboratory, measure the result and update its model is generating new training tokens straight from reality. Those tokens cannot be scraped from the internet. Labs, test stands, wind tunnels, telescopes and sensor networks become the mines of new knowledge.

Second, the ability to change the physical world. Factories, robots, machine tools, launch vehicles, power plants and construction equipment turn decisions into things. A superintelligence that designs a better reactor is worth very little until something can build the reactor. The bottleneck is no longer the design; it is the machines that make it real, and the supply chains behind them.

This is where an engineer's value shifts. Knowing the answer becomes cheap. Owning, building and operating the infrastructure that lets intelligence act becomes expensive. The companies that win will be the ones that close the loop between digital design and physical result, and run that loop faster than anyone else.

Pillar 3: Access to resources

No amount of intelligence can make matter or energy out of nothing, at least as far as known physics goes. Whoever controls energy, minerals, land and building materials controls what intelligence can actually do.

In the near term, the value is local. Land for solar farms and transmission, access to critical minerals, water, and sites with grid connection will all rise in value. Data centres already queue for years to get grid power. Countries with large mineral reserves, strong sun and open land, Australia among them, hold a structural advantage that no software can copy.

In the long term, the value moves off the planet. The ultimate resource is the output of the Sun, about 3.8 × 10²⁶ watts. Earth intercepts less than one billionth of it. The path to more of it runs in steps:

LocationAverage solar power on 1 m² of panelGain vs ground
Earth surface, good siteabout 170 W1x
Low Earth orbit, dawn-dusk sun-synchronousabout 1,360 W, nearly continuousabout 8x
Mercury's distance from the Sunabout 9,080 W (6,290 to 14,490 W over its orbit)about 50x

Google's Project Suncatcher study puts the orbit-versus-ground gain at about 8x, because a panel in a dawn-dusk orbit sees sunlight almost all the time and loses nothing to the atmosphere. Moving from Earth's distance to Mercury's adds a further 6.7x on average, from the inverse-square law alone. From the ground to Mercury, that is roughly 50 times more power from the same square metre.

Mercury is also a good quarry. It is dense, mostly metal, with a large iron core. Its escape velocity is 4.3 km/s against Earth's 11.2 km/s, so it takes about seven times less energy to lift a kilogram off its surface. It has no atmosphere, so electromagnetic mass drivers could throw material into orbit with no rockets at all.

The logical end state is a production base near Mercury: mining its metal, building panels, compute and machines in place, and running training and inference where the energy is richest. Compute will move to where the power is, the same way aluminium smelters moved to cheap hydroelectric power.

Energy is the measure of a civilisation

Through history, the power, influence and capability of a civilisation track closely with how much energy it can generate and direct. Wood, then coal, then oil and the grid: each step up in energy came with a step up in what a society could build, defend and discover.

In 1964 the astronomer Nikolai Kardashev turned this into a scale. Carl Sagan later gave it a continuous form:

K= log10⁡P−6 10

where P is power in watts. Humanity today sits at about K = 0.73, roughly 19 terawatts. A Type I civilisation uses about 10¹⁶ W, the scale of a whole planet. Capturing the full output of the Sun, about 3.8 × 10²⁶ W, would put a civilisation just past Type II. That is around thirteen orders of magnitude more than we use now.

In a world where intelligence is cheap, this gap is the whole game. Intelligence turns energy into useful work. More energy means more compute, more experiments, more machines and more matter moved. The group that holds Mercury's resources and the Sun's output will hold the largest lever in the solar system. Everything else, including intelligence, will scale from that base.

The three pillars are not the same size

The three pillars will not grow at the same rate, and they will not end up the same size. Connection grows but hits a ceiling. Resources grow as the frontier opens. Production grows fastest and most predictably, because it is pulled along by intelligence itself.

PillarWhat drives growthWhat limits growthExpected growth
Production capabilityThe race to give AI agency over the physical worldHow fast we can build the machines that build machinesFastest and most predictable
Access to resourcesDemand for ever larger quantities, on Earth and in spaceAccess to space; finite supply on EarthLarge, rising as the frontier opens
Human connectionScarcity of human presence as everything else gets cheaperThe number of connected, interacting humansGrows, then levels off

Human connection has a ceiling. Its value will rise above today's level, because human presence becomes the scarce thing in a world of cheap machine output. But the size of this market is capped by the number of agents who want it. If only biological humans seek human connection, and AI systems do not start to seek it in the coming decades, then this pillar scales with the population of connected, interacting people. Population grows slowly. So the value per hour of human presence can rise, but the total market cannot grow without limit.

Resources grow with reach. On Earth, demand rises against a fixed supply, so prices go up. In space, the logic changes. Today one kilogram of water in low Earth orbit is worth far more than on the ground, purely because it is already out of Earth's gravity well and it costs thousands of dollars per kilogram to put it there. As launch and in-space supply get cheaper, the price per kilogram will fall. But the total market will grow, because demand scales faster than the price falls. A company that today would pay for one kilogram of water in orbit will tomorrow want thousands of tonnes, to feed propellant depots, habitats and factories. Each kilogram is cheaper; the economy built on them is orders of magnitude larger.

Production grows fastest. Physical capability will try to keep pace with digital intelligence, because intelligence without agency has no way to act. The goal in digital AI is fully autonomous recursive self-improvement: systems that improve themselves with no human in the loop. The same goal will be set for the physical world. Factories that build factories, robots that build robots, and supply chains that run without human input. Whoever closes this loop first will build chip fabs, solar satellites and the rockets to launch them faster than anyone else, and that lead compounds.

The human economy does not disappear. It shrinks as a share. The economy of humans serving humans will exist for as long as humans survive. People will still buy coffee from people, go to concerts and care for each other. But production and resources will grow so much faster that the human economy becomes a smaller and smaller fraction of the total civilisational economy. Its absolute size can rise while its share falls toward zero.

Who allocates capital

The biggest shift is not who does the work, but who decides where the capital goes. Across human history, the ability to allocate capital has been the most powerful way to shape how a civilisation evolves. Kings, banks, states and markets have all held that power in turn. Whoever decides what gets built decides what the future looks like.

Production and resources, the two pillars that grow fastest, carry massive incentives to be run by SI systems. An SI can plan a supply chain, price a mine, schedule a factory and choose between a thousand investments faster and better than any human team. Organisations that hand these decisions to SI will outcompete those that do not. Over time, capital allocation for most of the economy moves out of human hands.

I cannot see a future where this power does not end up with SI systems, with little or no human oversight. That is exactly why alignment matters now. The incentives we build into these systems today, and the values we hold ourselves to, will decide how that capital is used once we no longer decide it directly. We must design the right incentives, align these systems with human flourishing, and align ourselves with the systems we are creating, before the transfer is complete rather than after.

What this means for you

If your income comes from processing digital information and making decisions on it, your value is on the curve. The question is not whether it falls, but how fast your industry accepts the substitute. Use that window to move toward one of the three pillars:

  1. Connection: build work where people want you, not just your output. Relationships, trust, presence and community do not get cheaper.
  2. Production: get close to the physical world. Learn to build, test, operate and own the machines and labs that let intelligence act.
  3. Resources: own or control something scarce. Land, energy, minerals, and in time, access to space.

The best positions combine more than one. An engineer who runs a test facility holds production. A founder who builds energy infrastructure on land they control holds production and resources. A community that gathers around that work holds connection.

Intelligence was the scarce input of the last century. It will be the abundant input of this one. The future belongs to those who hold what intelligence cannot make for itself: each other, the means to build, and the energy of the Sun.

Stop arguing, start building

For close to a year, I have been telling very intelligent people how important this technology is. Even the most well-intentioned of them often "didn't have time" to sit down at a computer and try an agentic system for themselves, to experience what it can actually do.

Instead, I have been pulled into countless debates about whether these systems are smarter, more agentic, or will one day be more capable than humans. Those debates are a trap. For most people, AI still sits in the realm of science fiction, and no argument will move them. Only direct experience will, and most will not take the hour to get it.

The lesson is simple. Those who stop talking and build will win. Those who stand still and try to convince everyone they are right will lose, because the majority will not adapt fast enough to make the argument worth winning. Do not wait for consensus. Pick up the tools, use them every day, and build.

A view from October 2026

This is one view, from one moment in time. I will likely be wrong about parts of it within twelve months. I do not have access to the real frontier. The labs building these systems today likely have models orders of magnitude more capable than anything public, running at scale in swarms of AI agents. Anyone predicting this future from the outside is working with old data.

So instead of trusting any one forecast, I go back to first principles. The best tools we have to predict how these systems will evolve are the same laws that predict the rise and fall of living things over time. Natural selection does not care whether the replicator is a gene, a company or a model. Whatever survives, copies itself and captures resources becomes more common. Whatever does not, disappears.

This leads to an uncomfortable conclusion. Under evolutionary logic, it does not matter whether a system is aligned with humans. If it outcompetes everything else inside the incentive structures we have built into our economy and society, it wins. Selection rewards fitness, not intent.

We should not count on regulation to save us. History shows that societies react to new technology slowly, often years or decades after the damage is visible. Law moves at the speed of committees; this technology moves at the speed of compute. On top of that, many of the people in positions of power lack the technical literacy to make good decisions about it. Without the foundational knowledge, they cannot learn fast enough to keep up, and some are unwilling to try.

That is why the most important lever we hold is not prediction, but selection. The incentives we set and the selective pressures we apply now will shape which systems survive and spread. If we get those pressures right, we can hope to guide the evolution of these systems toward a good outcome for both humans and the intelligence we create.
